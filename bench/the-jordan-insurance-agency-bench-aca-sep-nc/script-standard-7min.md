Rachel: So what is a Special Enrollment Period?

Billy: It's a window that lets you buy or change a Marketplace health plan outside of Open Enrollment. You get one when you have a qualifying life event, and for most events that window is sixty days. Miss it, and you're usually locked out of a comprehensive Marketplace plan until the next Open Enrollment, which runs November first through January fifteenth.

Rachel: [curious] What counts as a qualifying life event?

Billy: There are four buckets. The most common is losing qualifying coverage: losing job-based insurance after a layoff or reduced hours, turning twenty-six and aging off a parent's plan, running out of COBRA, or losing Medicaid. Second, household changes: marriage, a new baby, an adoption, or losing coverage after a divorce. Third, a permanent move, like moving to North Carolina from another state. And fourth, certain income and status changes that affect your eligibility for premium tax credits.

Rachel: Any catch with moving?

Billy: Yes. For a move, you generally have to show you had qualifying coverage for at least one of the sixty days before you moved. If you were uninsured that whole time, the move alone usually won't open the door.

Rachel: And what does not count?

Billy: Two big ones. Choosing to drop your own plan mid-year doesn't open a window. And if your policy gets canceled because you missed premium payments, that doesn't qualify you either.

Rachel: [thoughtfully] And if someone misses the window completely?

Billy: Then they usually have to wait for Open Enrollment, and a lot of people start looking at short-term or indemnity plans to fill the gap. I want to be careful here. Those plans typically don't meet the federal standards for comprehensive major medical coverage, and many exclude pre-existing conditions or leave out standard benefits. Terms vary a lot, so read the details with the carrier before buying one.

Rachel: Let's talk about the clock. Sixty days from what?

Billy: For most events, sixty days from the event itself: the wedding, the birth, the move. For losing coverage, it's better than people realize. You can enroll up to sixty days before the loss, and up to sixty days after.

Rachel: [surprised] Before you even lose your insurance?

Billy: Yes, and that's the most useful thing in this whole episode. If you know your job is ending or your employer is dropping its plan, pick your new plan ahead of time. Then your new coverage can start the first day of the month right after your old coverage ends. No gap.

Rachel: And if you wait?

Billy: Here's the example I give everybody. Your employer coverage ends May fifth. If you pick a plan on May sixth, your new policy usually doesn't start until June first. That's almost four weeks with no major medical coverage, for no reason except timing.

Rachel: How do start dates work for the other events?

Billy: For a marriage, pick a plan by the last day of the month and coverage starts the first of the following month. For a move, coverage starts the first of the month after you pick your plan. And if you're losing Medicaid, you get a longer window, ninety days after that coverage ends. But treat it like sixty and you'll never be caught short.

Rachel: Is there any event where coverage goes back in time?

Billy: [warmly] Yes, a new baby. For a birth, adoption, or foster placement, coverage goes back to the date of the event, so the delivery bills can be covered, as long as you enroll within the sixty days.

Rachel: Once someone picks a plan inside the window, are they done?

Billy: Not yet. After you select a plan, you generally have thirty days to send documents that prove your life event, and you can't use the coverage until your eligibility is confirmed and your first premium is paid. For a job loss, that's an official letter on the employer's or insurer's letterhead showing your name, your dependents, and the exact date coverage ends. For a move, a lease, mortgage statement, or utility bill, plus proof of your prior coverage.

Rachel: [curious] Why do documents get rejected?

Billy: [chuckles] Simple stuff. A blurry phone picture, a letter that isn't on letterhead, or a letter missing the exact end date. At The Jordan Insurance Agency, we look over those letters before they're uploaded, so they pass the first time.

Rachel: One more scenario. Someone leaves a corporate job to start their own business.

Billy: That's a qualifying event too, because you're losing employer coverage. Same rule: choose your plan before your last day of employer coverage. And don't only compare premiums. Check the network. Around Charlotte, most care runs through Atrium Health and Novant Health, so make sure the plan you pick keeps your doctors in network. And if your income changes, update your application on HealthCare.gov so your premium tax credit is recalculated. Otherwise you can get a surprise at tax time when you reconcile on IRS Form 8962.
